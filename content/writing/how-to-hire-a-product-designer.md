_You have a product, a budget and no designer. This is how I would hire one if I were on your side of the table._

I have been the designer on the other end of this decision since 2013.

This guide is the order I would do it in. It takes about a week from the first search to a signed scope, and most of that week is waiting for replies.

## Decide what you are hiring for

"I need a designer" is four different jobs. Pick one before you message anyone, because each one needs a different person and a different budget.

**A new product from zero.** You have an idea, maybe a spec, and no screens. You need flows first, then screens, then a component library your developers can build from. This is the largest job and it is where a product designer earns the title.

**A redesign of something live.** People already use it. The designer has to start from what is built, keep what users rely on and fix what loses them. Ask for someone who has redesigned a live product before, because the skill is knowing what not to touch.

**One feature or one flow.** Onboarding, checkout, a new AI assistant, a reporting screen. A focused week or two. This is the cheapest way to test a designer before a bigger commitment.

**A fix for an app you built with AI tools.** It works, and it looks like every other app built with Cursor, Lovable or v0. That is not a redesign. It is a pass over spacing, type, color, states and copy, and it is quick when the designer has done it before. I wrote up [the 25 fixes](/writing/ai-slop-ui-fixes/) if you want to try it yourself first.

Write your one job down in a sentence. "Redesign the onboarding of our scheduling app so new accounts reach their first booking." If you cannot write the sentence, you are not ready to hire, and a 30-minute call with a designer is the fastest way to get it written.

## Freelancer, agency or in-house

The short version:

- **A freelancer** is one senior person who does the work themselves. Fastest to start, lowest cost, and the person on the call is the person in the file. The risk is that one person has one calendar.
- **An agency** is a team with a project manager. More hands and more process. You pay for the layers, and the person who sold you the project is rarely the one designing it.
- **An in-house designer** makes sense once design work is constant, every week, for a year or more. Before that you are paying a salary for a role that is busy half the time.

Most SaaS and AI products under about 20 people are best served by a freelancer or a small studio until the design work never stops. I compare the first two in detail in [freelance designer vs agency](/writing/freelance-designer-vs-agency/).

## Know the price range before you ask

Prices vary more than founders expect. Figma's own cost guide puts it this way: a freelancer might come in at $4,000 and an agency might propose $35,000 for what reads like the same deliverables on paper.

Three public reference points, all checked in November 2026:

- Upwork lists product designers at $20 to $50 an hour.
- Clutch says most UX agencies charge $25 to $49 an hour, and that UX projects on Clutch are typically more than $10,000.
- Figma's guide cites a US Bureau of Labor Statistics median salary of $104,000 a year for digital designers.

Those hourly figures are averages across the whole world and every skill level. A senior product designer who has shipped SaaS products usually charges more, and is usually cheaper in total, because they need fewer rounds.

My own projects start at $5,000, with a fixed price in writing before work starts, or a $2,500 trial week if you want to see how I work first. [The design cost calculator](/tools/design-cost-calculator/) gives you a rough range in a minute.

Ask for a fixed price and a timeline in writing. Hourly billing with no ceiling moves all the risk to you.

## Where to look

In the order I would try them:

1. **Ask two founders whose product you like.** "Who designed this?" is the best search query there is. A referral comes with a product you can already click through.
2. **Look at the product, then find the designer.** When an app feels clear, check the footer, the launch post and the founder's posts. Designers get credited more often than you would think.
3. **Portfolio sites.** Dribbble and personal sites show taste. They do not show whether the work shipped, so use them to build a shortlist and nothing more.
4. **Marketplaces.** Upwork, Contra and Toptal give you contracts, payments and reviews in one place. The reviews are the useful part. Read the three-star ones.
5. **Search for the exact job.** "SaaS dashboard designer" or "designer for AI app" finds people who specialise. A designer who has written about your problem has thought about it for longer than one project.

Shortlist five. Message three. You are not looking for the best designer in the world. You are looking for one who has solved your kind of problem and can start this month.

## Read a portfolio in ten minutes

Pretty screens are the minimum. These five checks tell you more.

1. **Is there a product like yours?** A dashboard designer and a brand designer are different trades. If you are building a B2B web app, look for dense screens: tables, filters, settings, forms.
2. **Do the case studies show the boring screens?** Empty states, error messages, loading, the settings page. Anyone can design a hero screen. The boring screens are where a product is won.
3. **Is there a reason behind each decision?** "We moved the total above the chart because that is the first question people ask" is a designer. "Clean, modern, minimal" is a mood board.
4. **Is real data in the mockups?** Long names, zeros, 10,000 rows. Design made on perfect data breaks the day it meets yours.
5. **Is it labelled honestly?** Concept work is fine, and good designers do a lot of it. It should say concept. If every project claims a 300% lift with no client named, be careful.

## Eight questions for the first call

A 30-minute call is enough.

1. **"What would you need from us in week one?"** A good answer is a list: access, sample data, the three users to talk to, the metrics you watch. A bad answer is "nothing, I'll just start".
2. **"Walk me through one project from brief to handover."** Listen for the middle. Everyone can describe the start and the end.
3. **"What did you get wrong on a past project?"** People who have shipped have a real answer ready.
4. **"Who does the work?"** With a freelancer it should be the person you are talking to. With an agency, ask to meet that person before you sign.
5. **"What do my developers receive?"** You want organized files, components with every state, and notes they can build from. Ask whether the designer can build the front end too, if you are short on developers.
6. **"How do we handle changes?"** Scope will move. You want to hear how a change gets priced and agreed, before it happens.
7. **"What happens if we disagree on a design?"** The right answer involves evidence: a test, a user call, the numbers. Not "you're the client" and not "trust me".
8. **"What would you not do for this budget?"** Honest designers cut scope out loud. The rest say yes to everything and cut it quietly later.

Notice what they ask you. A designer who asks about your users, your numbers and what happens after signup is already working. One who only asks about colors and references is decorating.

## Start with one paid week

Do not ask for free test work. Good designers will not do it, and the ones who will are telling you something.

Buy one week instead. Pick the single flow that hurts most, usually onboarding or the main screen, and pay for a focused week on it. You see how they think, how they communicate and what they hand over, on your real product, for a small fixed cost. If it goes well you have a finished flow and a designer who already knows the product. If it does not, you have lost a week and learned it cheaply.

## Write the brief

A one-page brief gets you better quotes and fewer surprises. Copy this and fill it in:

```
PRODUCT: what it does, in one sentence.
USERS: who uses it and what they come to get done.
THE JOB: new product, redesign, one flow, or a fix for an AI-built app.
THE PROBLEM: what is going wrong today, with a number if you have one.
SCOPE: the screens or flows you want designed. List them.
WHAT EXISTS: live product link, Figma files, brand, code stack.
WHO BUILDS IT: your developers, an agency, or you need the build too.
DEADLINE: the date and what is driving it.
BUDGET: a range. Designers scope to it, they do not just spend it.
DECISION MAKER: who signs off designs. One name.
```

The budget line is the one founders leave out. Give a range anyway. A good designer will tell you what fits inside it and what does not.

## Red flags

- No questions about your users or your numbers.
- A quote within the hour, before seeing the product.
- "Unlimited revisions." It means nobody has thought about how decisions get made.
- A portfolio of landing pages when you need a product.
- Reviews or results that cannot be traced to anyone.
- No written scope. If it is not written down, it is not agreed.

## What the first two weeks should look like

Week one: a kickoff call, access to the product and data, and a first pass at the main flow by the end of the week. Rough is fine. Silent is not.

Week two: screens in progress that you can click through, a short list of open questions, and the first decisions already made together. If you reach day ten and have seen nothing, say so. Good designers show work early because early feedback is cheap.

## Make the first month work

Hiring well is half of it. The other half is on your side.

- **Give access on day one.** The product, the analytics, the old Figma files, a login with real data. Every day a designer waits for access is a day you paid for.
- **Answer within a day.** Design moves at the speed of your replies. A question that sits for a week costs a week.
- **Give reasons, not fixes.** "This feels crowded" is useful. "Make the button blue and move it left" is you doing the design. Say what is wrong and let the designer solve it.
- **Put your developers in the room early.** A ten-minute look at the first screens saves a rebuild later.
- **Agree what done means.** Screens, states, components, notes for the build. Write the list at the start and tick it off at the end.

## Questions people ask

### How long does it take to hire a product designer?

About a week if you are organised: two days to shortlist, two or three days of calls, then a written scope. An in-house hire takes far longer, which is one reason early teams start with a freelancer.

### Should I hire a product designer or a UI designer?

If you need someone to decide what the screens should do, hire a product designer. If the flows are settled and you need them to look right, a UI designer is enough. Most early products need the first.

### How much does a product designer cost for a SaaS app?

Public marketplaces list $20 to $50 an hour and agencies average $25 to $49 an hour, with senior specialists above that. For project work, expect anything from a few thousand dollars for one flow to $25,000 or more for a full product. My own rates are on the pricing page.

### Do I need a designer if I built my app with AI?

For a first version, often not. Once strangers are using it and leaving, yes. The [designer vs vibe coding](/writing/designer-vs-vibe-coding/) page covers where the line is.

### Can one designer handle both the product and the marketing site?

Often, yes, and it keeps the two consistent. Ask to see both kinds of work in the portfolio, because they are different skills.
